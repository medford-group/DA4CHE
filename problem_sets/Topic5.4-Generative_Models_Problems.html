Setup#

This set uses the carbon XPS spectra from the Clustering problem set. You do not need to have done that set first; what you need from it is below.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy measures the binding energy of core electrons, which shifts as the atom’s chemical environment changes. For carbon, the C 1s line moves to higher binding energy as the carbon becomes more oxidized:

data/xps_carbon_monitoring.csv holds 520 simulated C 1s spectra from 280 to 295 eV in 0.05 eV steps, in two batches:

  • batch == 'library' — 400 reference spectra, 100 from each of four surface chemistries, with the true class in label (0–3) and chemistry. Every spectrum is a mixture: a dominant component plus adventitious hydrocarbon and some of the neighboring oxidation states, so the four classes overlap.

  • batch == 'new' — 120 spectra from a later campaign on the same instrument. Nobody has looked at them yet, so label and chemistry are blank. They are the subject of Part C.

Parts A and B use only the library.

The preprocessing is the one from the Clustering set, and is given here. It subtracts a linear background drawn between the means of the first and last 20 channels, then scales each spectrum to sum to 100, so that each channel is a percentage of that sample’s total signal. The density models in Parts A and B are fit to the first three PCA scores, Z_lib, rather than to all 301 channels. The Clustering set’s A3 asks why three is enough.

def preprocess(raw):
    """Linear background subtraction, then scale every spectrum to sum to 100."""
    bg = np.linspace(raw[:, :20].mean(axis=1), raw[:, -20:].mean(axis=1), raw.shape[1]).T
    sub = raw - bg
    return 100.0 * sub / sub.sum(axis=1)[:, None]

X_lib = preprocess(lib[ecols].values)
X_new = preprocess(new[ecols].values)

pca = PCA(n_components=3, random_state=0).fit(X_lib)   # fit to the library only
Z_lib = pca.transform(X_lib)
Z_new = pca.transform(X_new)
print(f"Z_lib {Z_lib.shape}, Z_new {Z_new.shape}")

Z_lib (400, 3), Z_new (120, 3)

Part B — Visualization (35 pts)#

The clustering set ended with an unanswered question. k-means recovered the four chemistries with a purity well short of 1 at \(k = 4\), and it was not clear whether to blame the clustering or the data. A generative classifier can settle it. Fit a density to each chemistry separately, using the labels this time, and combine them with Bayes’ theorem as in the chapter’s not-so-naive Bayes section. If even that classifier cannot do much better than the clustering did, the classes overlap in the data, and no clustering algorithm could have separated them.

The second question is about the other thing a generative model is for. A3’s mixture is a model of the library’s density, and you can draw new samples from it. Do the samples look like XPS spectra?

Part C — Open Ended (35 pts)#

Now the new batch. The instrument’s log shows three things went wrong at some point during the campaign, and nobody recorded which spectra were affected:

  • Sample charging. An insulating sample charged up, shifting its whole spectrum to higher binding energy by an unknown amount.

  • Fluoropolymer contamination. PTFE tape was used on the sample holder for part of the campaign. Fluorinated carbon (CF\(_2\)) appears near 292 eV, where no library spectrum has a peak.

  • A new oxidation treatment. A few samples came from a harsher treatment than anything in the library.

Your job is to find the affected spectra using only the library as a reference. The mixture from A3 is a natural first tool, but it has a blind spot: it only ever sees the three PCA scores. Anything a spectrum does outside those three directions is removed by the projection before the density model sees it. The squared prediction error from the Dimensionality Reduction problem set measures exactly that part:

\[ \text{SPE} = \lVert \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} \rVert^2, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{x}} = \texttt{pca\_k.inverse\_transform}(\texttt{pca\_k.transform}(\mathbf{x})) \]

where pca_k is a PCA with \(k\) components fit to X_lib. So the two alarms ask different questions: the density alarm asks whether a spectrum sits in a typical place inside the library’s subspace, and the residual alarm asks whether it sits in that subspace at all.

Exercise 5

1. Density alarm. Score Z_new with A3’s mixture and flag every new spectrum whose log-probability is below ll_limit. Plot histograms of the library’s and the new batch’s log-probabilities on one set of axes with the threshold marked, and report how many new spectra are flagged.

2. Residual alarm. For \(k = 3, 4, \ldots, 10\), fit PCA(k, random_state=0) to X_lib and set the SPE limit at the 99th percentile of the library’s SPE. Plot the limit against \(k\), choose a \(k\), and explain the choice in one sentence. Then report how many new spectra are above the limit at your \(k\).

3. Diagnose. Sort the flagged spectra into three groups: flagged by the density alarm only, by the residual alarm only, and by both. For each flagged spectrum, decide which of the three incidents explains it, or whether it is a false alarm, using evidence from the spectrum itself. Report the count for each diagnosis. Plotting a few of each group against the library’s mean spectrum is a good place to start.

4. Recommend. In three sentences, tell the lab how to screen future batches. Say which alarm or combination of alarms to use, which incident your screen still misses and why, and how many false alarms they should expect per 100 ordinary spectra. Use your numbers.

There is more than one defensible diagnosis method. You are graded on whether your evidence supports each diagnosis.
